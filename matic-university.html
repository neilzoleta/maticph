What does a Revenue Manager actually do?
The core job, in one paragraph
Role ›

A Revenue Manager decides what price a property charges on which night — and ensures that price is correct everywhere it's listed. The goal is to maximise revenue by finding the sweet spot: high enough to capture what guests will pay, low enough to actually fill the calendar.

At a company like RevTech managing 312+ properties, you're not doing this manually for each one. You're setting intelligent pricing rules in tools like PriceLabs, monitoring performance dashboards for problems, and intervening when something breaks — a sync failure, an underperforming market, a pricing anomaly.

Think of it like airline pricing. The same seat costs different amounts depending on when you book, how full the flight is, and the day. A Revenue Manager does the same for rental properties — charging more during peak demand, less during slow periods, and adjusting in real time as bookings come in. Except you're doing it for hundreds of properties across multiple platforms at once.
🌐
OTAs — Airbnb, Booking.com, and why they differ
The platforms guests use to find properties
Term ›

OTA stands for Online Travel Agency. These are marketplaces — Airbnb, Booking.com, Expedia, Vrbo — where travellers search for accommodation and make bookings. Property owners list their properties and pay a commission (usually 3–20%) per confirmed booking.

Think of OTAs like Lazada or Shopee. You're a seller listing products for shoppers to find. The marketplace takes a cut of every sale. Your job is to make sure your listings show up, look good, and are priced competitively — just like optimising a product listing.

Airbnb vs Booking.com — key differences you must know:

Rate plansSimple (1 rate type)Complex (Standard, Non-refundable, Early Booker, Mobile)

PMS — the central brain of a property business
Property Management System, explained simply
Tool ›

A Property Management System (PMS) is the central software platform that manages everything about a rental property portfolio: reservations, guest details, check-in/check-out schedules, cleaning tasks, pricing rules, and connections to all booking platforms. Everything flows through it.

Think of PMS like a restaurant's POS system. A restaurant's POS tracks every order, table, payment, and inventory item. Nothing happens without going through it. A PMS does the same for properties — every booking, guest, rate change, and platform sync runs through the PMS. Popular examples: Guesty, Hostaway, Hospitable, Lodgify.

This is your most important knowledge gap. You've been working directly in Airbnb's interface and PriceLabs. Professional property management companies add a PMS layer between those tools and the OTAs. When you change a price, it happens in the PMS — which then pushes the update to Airbnb and Booking.com through the channel manager.

How to get hands-on this week

  • 2
    During the demo, focus on three things: how properties are added, where rates live, and how channels (Airbnb, Booking.com) are connected and synced.
  • 3
    Ask this specific question: "If PriceLabs updates a rate for next Saturday, walk me through exactly how that change reaches the guest on Airbnb." Understanding this flow cold is interview-critical.
  • 4
    After the demo, draw the flow on paper from memory: PriceLabs → PMS → Channel Manager → Airbnb/Booking.com. If you can't draw it cleanly, watch the demo again.

Channel Manager — keeping all platforms in sync
The software that connects your property to multiple OTAs at once
Tool ›

A channel manager connects a property to multiple OTAs simultaneously and keeps prices, availability, and restrictions in sync across all of them. When a booking comes in on Airbnb, the channel manager instantly closes those dates on Booking.com and Expedia — preventing a guest on Booking.com from also booking the same dates.

Think of it like a social media scheduler. Instead of logging into Instagram, Facebook, and Twitter separately and posting the same thing three times, a tool like Buffer posts to all of them simultaneously. A channel manager does the same for property listings — one price change goes everywhere at once.

The job spec mentions Hostify and RMS specifically. The most critical thing to understand: sync accuracy is a hard KPI (99%). A misconfigured channel manager means guests on different platforms see different — or wrong — prices. A double-booking is one of the worst failures in this role. Your job includes ensuring connections are set up correctly and monitoring for sync failures.

Common sync failure causes to know: mismatched rate plan IDs, API connection drops, manually overriding prices on the OTA side (bypassing the channel manager), and incorrect minimum stay mapping between PMS and OTA.

📋
Rate plans — the different pricing packages you offer guests
Why one property can have multiple prices at once
Term ›

A rate plan is a specific combination of: a price + a set of conditions (cancellation policy, booking window, guest type). The same property can simultaneously offer multiple rate plans to attract different types of guests.

Think of it like airline fare classes. Economy, business, first class are different products. But even within economy there's a flexible fare (refundable, costs more) and a saver fare (non-refundable, costs less). Hotels and STRs do exactly the same thing.
Rate PlanWhat it isBest for
Standard / FlexibleFull price, free cancellationGuests who want flexibility, peak demand periods
Non-refundableLower price, no cancellationLocking in revenue early, shoulder season
Early BookerDiscount for booking 30–90 days outFilling calendars early, securing cash flow
Last-MinuteDiscount for bookings 1–7 days outFilling gaps that would otherwise stay empty
Mobile RateBooking.com app-only discountImproving Booking.com search ranking

Rate plan mapping (in channel manager settings) is how you tell the system: "my Standard rate in the PMS should appear as the Flexible rate on Booking.com." Getting this wrong is a serious, hard-to-detect error.

📅
Minimum stay restrictions — protecting revenue by controlling bookings
Why a 1-night booking can actually hurt you
Strategy ›

A minimum stay restriction requires guests to book at least X nights to be eligible. For example, a "2-night minimum on weekends" rule means you won't accept a Saturday-only booking. This is a core revenue protection strategy, not just a convenience preference.

The problem a single-night booking creates: Imagine Friday and Sunday are free. Someone books just Saturday. Now you have two orphan nights on either side — Friday and Sunday — that are very hard to sell because most guests won't book a 1-night stay mid-week (or they'd need to arrive and leave on unusual days). A 2-night minimum forces the guest to take Friday+Saturday or Saturday+Sunday, protecting your total revenue.

The art is in knowing when to relax restrictions. If a weekend is 6 weeks out and barely filling, keeping a 3-night minimum will leave it empty. Dropping to 2 nights — or even 1 — might fill it. Booking pace is what tells you when to adjust. PriceLabs can automate minimum stay rules based on how far in advance a date falls and how full surrounding dates are.

🏙️
Comp-set — your benchmarking group of competitor properties
How you know if your prices are right
Strategy ›

A comp-set (competitive set) is the group of similar properties you use as your benchmark. If you manage a 2-bedroom condo in BGC, your comp-set might be 8–10 other 2-bedroom condos in BGC with similar amenities and ratings. You track what they charge, how full they are, and use that data to validate your own pricing decisions.

Think of it like salary benchmarking. You don't pick a salary number out of thin air — you check what similar roles at similar companies pay. A comp-set does this for nightly rates. If your comps are all charging ₱8,000 and you're at ₱12,000 with zero bookings, that's a signal that needs investigation.

How to choose a valid comp-set (not just nearby properties):

  • 1
    Same property type (apartment, villa, studio) and similar size (bedrooms/bathrooms)
  • 2
    Similar quality tier — comparable amenities and review scores (don't comp a 4.9★ against a 4.2★)
  • 3
    Same target guest — a digital nomad property competes differently from a family vacation home even in the same neighbourhood
  • 4
    5–12 properties is the right size. Too few = noisy data. Too many = diluted signal.

Tools like AirDNA, Wheelhouse, and PriceLabs' Market Dashboard help you build and track comp-sets automatically.

Module 3 — Metrics & KPIs

The numbers
that run the role

Every metric in the job spec defined with its formula, what it tells you, and what to do when it's off.

The core three

ADR, Occupancy, and RevPAR

These three metrics are the foundation of everything. Learn to calculate them in your sleep — they'll come up in every interview and every client report.

ADR
Revenue ÷ Nights Booked
Average Daily Rate — how much you earned per booked night
Occupancy
Booked ÷ Available × 100
Percentage of nights that were actually filled
RevPAR
ADR × Occupancy %
Revenue Per Available night — the master metric
Worked example

Property earned ₱84,000 in October. Available nights: 31. Booked nights: 24.

ADR = ₱84,000 ÷ 24 = ₱3,500
Revenue divided by nights actually booked
Occupancy = 24 ÷ 31 = 77.4%
Booked nights as a percentage of available nights
RevPAR = ₱3,500 × 77.4% = ₱2,709
The number that captures both price AND fill rate — the master metric
Why RevPAR beats ADR alone: A property charging ₱6,000/night at 40% occupancy earns RevPAR of ₱2,400. A property charging ₱3,000 at 90% earns RevPAR of ₱2,700. RevPAR correctly identifies the second property as the better performer. ADR alone would mislead you.
Operational metrics

The signals you monitor daily

⏱️
Booking pace — your most important daily signal
How fast a future date is filling up
Metric ›

Booking pace is how quickly reservations are filling up for a future date, compared to what you'd normally expect at this point in the booking window. It's the earliest warning signal in revenue management — it tells you whether to raise prices or take action to fill a slow period, weeks before the date arrives.

Concert ticket analogy: If Taylor Swift tickets usually last 3 days before selling out, but this time they're gone in 2 hours — that's ahead of pace. The promoter should have charged more. Revenue managers watch booking pace to catch these signals early and adjust prices before demand passes.
Pace SignalWhat it meansYour action
Ahead of paceFilling faster than historical norm for this dateRaise prices — demand is strong
On paceFilling at expected rateHold current pricing strategy
Behind paceFilling slower than historical normConsider price reduction, min-stay relaxation, or promotions

You can track booking pace manually (check how many of 30 available nights are booked each week vs. the same time last year) or use PriceLabs' built-in pace alerts. At portfolio scale, pace alerts are essential — you can't manually check 312 properties daily.

👁️
Conversion rate — distinguishing pricing problems from visibility problems
% of listing views that turn into bookings
Metric ›

Conversion rate = bookings ÷ listing views × 100. If 200 people viewed a listing and 4 booked, conversion = 2%.

Why it matters: low bookings can come from two very different causes — a pricing problem or a visibility problem. Conversion rate tells you which one.

PatternDiagnosisFix
Views high, bookings lowPricing or listing quality issue — people are finding you but not bookingReview pricing vs comp-set; check listing photos, description, reviews
Views low, bookings lowVisibility / ranking issue — not enough people are finding youCheck OTA ranking signals: response rate, review score, completion rate
Views high, bookings highHealthy — possibly underpriced if pace is well aheadConsider raising prices to capture more revenue per booking
🗓️
Lead time & booking window — when guests are booking relative to arrival
Understanding your demand pattern
Metric ›

Lead time is how far in advance of their arrival date guests are booking. A 14-day average lead time means guests book 14 days before check-in. The booking window is the distribution of all your lead times — showing the mix of far-ahead vs. last-minute bookers.

Lead time patternWhat it signalsStrategy implication
Mostly 0–7 daysSpontaneous market — guests decide lateKeep prices higher until closer to date, use aggressive last-minute discounts to fill gaps
Mostly 30–90 daysPlanners market — guests book far aheadOffer early-booker discounts to secure revenue, price up as the date approaches
MixedStandard leisure marketLayered approach: hold for planned bookers, discount last-7-days gaps
📊
RevPAR Index (RGI) — how you're performing vs. competitors
The competitive benchmarking metric
Metric ›

RevPAR Index (also called Revenue Generating Index or RGI) compares your RevPAR to your comp-set's average RevPAR. It tells you whether you're winning or losing against the market — regardless of whether the market itself is up or down.

RGI = Your RevPAR ÷ Comp-set Average RevPAR
Above 1.0 = outperforming market. Below 1.0 = underperforming market.

Example: Your property has RevPAR of ₱2,800. Your comp-set averages ₱2,400. RGI = 1.17 — you're outperforming the market by 17%.

This is a more meaningful metric than RevPAR alone. If all properties in the market fell 20% due to a slow season, a flat RevPAR means you underperformed — your RGI dropped below 1.0. Understanding this distinction is what separates a strategic RM from a basic one.

Module 5 — Strategy Skills

The advisory
layer of the role

Revenue management isn't just adjusting numbers — it's diagnosing problems and recommending solutions. These are the strategic frameworks you need.

Framework 1

Diagnosing underperformance — the right order

The most common mistake is prescribing before diagnosing. A property with low revenue could have a dozen different causes — and the wrong fix makes things worse.

  • 1
    Check views vs. bookings separately. Low views = visibility/ranking problem. High views + low bookings = pricing or listing quality problem. These need completely different fixes.
  • 2
    Check booking pace vs. historical. Is this property behind pace for its market and season, or is the whole market slow? If it's market-wide, don't over-discount — you're fighting macroeconomic headwinds, not a property-specific problem.
  • 3
    Check pricing vs. comp-set. Are you significantly above or below competitors for the same dates? A 30% premium over comps needs justification (better amenities, better reviews). If you can't justify it, it's causing the underperformance.
  • 4
    Check minimum stay restrictions. Are restrictions creating orphan gaps? A Monday–Thursday gap that no one will book because of a 2-night minimum is a restriction problem, not a pricing problem.
  • 5
    Check sync accuracy. Are prices actually showing correctly on all OTAs? A misconfigured rate plan could mean your discounted price isn't being displayed. Verify the end-to-end before concluding it's a demand problem.
Framework 2

Cancellation policy decision tree

ContextRecommended policyReasoning
Peak season, high demand marketStrict or Non-refundableGuests will book regardless. Protect revenue from last-minute cancellations when you can't rebook.
Shoulder season, moderate demandModerateBalance between protecting revenue and attracting bookings. Not so strict that guests choose elsewhere.
Low season, soft marketFlexibleRemove as many friction points as possible. You need bookings more than you need cancellation protection.
New listing building reviewsFlexibleNew listings need to accumulate bookings and reviews. Friction = death for a new listing.
Long-stay focused propertyModerate to Strict with sliding scaleLonger stays represent more revenue at risk from cancellation. Justified protection as stay length increases.
Framework 3

Pricing curve strategy by booking window

Price should not be static. It changes based on how far away the date is and how fast it's filling. Here's the standard approach.

90+ days out

Hold at base price or slight premium. You have time — don't discount early. Early booker rates can be offered as a specific rate plan to capture planners without lowering your standard rate.

30–90 days out

Monitor pace weekly. If ahead of pace, raise prices. If on pace, hold. If behind pace, evaluate: is it a price issue or demand issue? Don't panic-discount without diagnosing.

7–30 days out

Make active decisions. Empty calendar at 3 weeks out needs action: reduce minimum stay, add last-minute rate, or consider promotional pricing. Don't wait until the week before.

0–7 days out

Last-minute window. An empty night has zero recoverable value once it passes. A deeply discounted night still generates cleaning fee + some margin. Be aggressive on empty gaps — but not so aggressive you train your market to always wait for last-minute deals.

Maintaining 99% sync accuracy

How to keep the hardest KPI clean

  • 1
    Never manually override prices directly in an OTA. If you change a price in Airbnb's dashboard directly, it bypasses the channel manager. The next automated sync will overwrite it — or not, creating a permanent mismatch. Always make price changes in PriceLabs or the PMS.
  • 2
    Review channel manager sync logs weekly. Look for failed syncs, partial updates, or timestamps that suggest a delay. Catch problems in hours, not weeks.
  • 3
    Do a spot-check audit monthly. Pick 10 random property-date combinations. Check what PriceLabs shows vs. what a guest would see on Airbnb and Booking.com. These should match within rounding. If they don't, you have a sync configuration problem to investigate.
  • 4
    Document rate plan mapping per property. Keep a record of which PMS rate type maps to which Booking.com rate plan for every property. When a new property is onboarded, this checklist prevents the most common error.
  • 5
    Set up API connection health alerts. Most channel managers have connection monitoring — configure alerts so you're notified within hours if a channel connection drops, not days later when a guest finds a wrong price.
